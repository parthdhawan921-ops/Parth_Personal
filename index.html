Cart leakage and repeat cohorts

Question
How much revenue leaks between add-to-cart and purchase, which categories cause it, and what is a cart-reminder nudge worth?
Answer
About $3.93M of cart value goes unbought each month. Half of it sits in just 23 of 465 categories. A nudge recovering 10% is worth about $91K a month net.
Data
20.7M clickstream events from an online cosmetics store, Oct 2019 to Feb 2020.
Code and memo

23 / 465categories hold half the leak
9.2%of new buyers come back the next month
~$91Ka month from a reminder at 10% recovery

Leak of $3.93M a month × recovery × margin, minus about 0.69M reminders a month at $0.01 each. Matches the project's figures: 5% → $42K, 10% → $91K, 15% → $140K. Recovery and margin are assumptions, so the memo recommends a holdout test to measure the real rate.

Method
  1. Loaded five monthly CSVs into Delta tables on Databricks.
  2. Profiled and cleaned: removed duplicates and invalid prices (19.50M of 20.69M events kept).
  3. Built the view → cart → purchase funnel by users and by sessions.
  4. Defined an abandoned cart as no purchase of that product within 7 days, excluding the final 7 days of data.
  5. Valued the leak by month, brand and category with a Pareto analysis.
  6. Built first-purchase cohorts and priced the nudge from stated assumptions.
Limits I flagged
  • Recovery rate, margin and reminder cost are assumed, not measured.
  • 40.1% of abandoned value has no brand recorded.
  • The October cohort looks inflated because the data starts that month.

Who should get the email?

Question
Send the men's email, the women's email, or nothing, and to which customers, to make the most extra profit?
Answer
Send the men's email to everyone, with a small holdout. It earns about $131 extra profit per 1,000 customers. A machine-learning targeting model earned less ($73) in every scenario tested.
Data
A public randomised email experiment with 64,000 customers.
Notebook
+$0.77spend per customer from the men's email (95% CI $0.50 to $1.07)
10.6→18.3%site visit rate, no email vs men's email
22 / 38segments still significant after Benjamini-Hochberg
−44%profit if we had trusted the targeting model

Make the call first, then see the result

30% margin, $0.10 per email

Which plan would you choose?
Pick one. Most people expect the model to win.
Extra profit per 1,000 customers

Whisker on the men's email shows the 95% bootstrap range ($42 to $211). Targeting lost because the men's email lifts almost every kind of customer, and only about 0.9% buy within two weeks, so per-person predictions are noisy.

Method
  1. Checked the test was fair: sample-ratio test (chi-square 0.2) and a balance table with every gap under 0.01 SD.
  2. Measured lift with t-tests and a 2,000-round bootstrap. Minimum detectable effect: $0.31 per customer.
  3. Tested 38 segments with permutation p-values and Benjamini-Hochberg correction.
  4. Trained a T-learner uplift model (gradient boosting, 5-fold CV) and checked it with a Qini curve.
  5. Valued each policy with inverse-probability weighting and a margin × cost sensitivity table.
Limits I flagged
  • Only two weeks of outcomes after the send.
  • No unsubscribe or complaint data, so list fatigue is not priced in.
  • Margin and email cost are assumptions, tested in a sensitivity table.

Dark-store break-even model

Question
How many orders a day does a quick-commerce dark store need to break even, and which lever moves it furthest?
Answer
About 1,133 orders a day, 5.4% above today's ~1,075. Basket size is the strongest lever: +10% adds about ₹2.4 lakh per store per month, roughly ₹85 crore a quarter across 1,171 stores.
Data
Swiggy Instamart's Q1 FY27 shareholder letter and KPI databook (Apr to Jun 2026).
Workbook
114.5Morders in the quarter, ₹691 average order value
~1,133orders per store per day to break even
−0.2%contribution margin as disclosed
₹85 crper quarter from a 10% bigger basket

Fixed cost is calibrated so the model reproduces the disclosed −0.2% margin (about ₹8.6 lakh per store per month). Cost split, fees, ad income and discounts are my assumptions, not disclosures. Inputs move one at a time here; in reality basket size, volume and discounts affect each other. Discount war shows volume up and margin down, because every extra order carries a deeper discount.

Method
  1. Rebuilt the quarter per order and per store per day: ~1,074.5 orders, 15.6% take rate.
  2. Split costs into variable (rider, picking, packaging, payment, wastage) and fixed (rent, staff, power).
  3. Calibrated product margin to the disclosed ₹108 revenue per order, and fixed cost to the disclosed margin.
  4. Break-even = fixed cost per day ÷ contribution per order (~₹25.42).
  5. Built live sensitivity grids, four scenarios, a tornado chart and a Checks sheet that must read ALL PASS.
Limits I flagged
  • Public data gives only total cost, so the variable / fixed split is an assumption.
  • One quarter of company-wide averages; single stores will differ.
  • Benchmarked against the market leader only as a sanity check.

Design rules I wrote for it
  1. Code computes, the AI only explains. Every number comes from a script.
  2. I own the definitions: metrics and quality rules are written in plain text and the AI must follow them.
  3. Trust layer before reports: a failed check stops the run, and a bad run never overwrites a good dashboard.
  4. If a task repeats, it becomes a reusable skill (9 so far).
Problems it found in the data
  • Two markets (Spain, Austria, 13% of revenue) missing from the company docs.
  • Dates in two formats and stock months without leading zeros.
  • Built on course practice data, so the business is fictional; the method is real.
